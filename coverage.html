GM Patch Coverage

All 128 programs, rated by how much reality has been allowed to contradict the model — from a shared base standing in, through a voice derived from how the instrument works, to one fitted against a recording of the thing itself.

The whole set at once

General MIDI’s program list is exactly sixteen families of eight, so the whole of it fits in one grid. Each cell is a program, coloured by its rating; a dashed outline marks a patch played by a voice of the wrong physical kind. The rest of the specification is not a list of sounds but a list of behaviours — how many voices, which channel is percussion, which controllers must respond — and that half is documented in midi.md rather than here.

Where it stands

32 27 69
  • 0nothingno voice at all
  • 1general classa shared base, or another instrument standing in
  • 2specific, theoryits own voice, derived from how the instrument works
  • 3specific, theory + ear...and the numbers moved after listening
  • 4specific, reference audio...and fitted against a recording of the instrument

Nothing scores 0, because every program resolves to something — which is not the same as every program being served. The line that matters is the one at 4: audio of the instrument, analysed and fitted against. Published measurements that are not audio — the Rhodes’ high-speed-camera papers, the free reeds’ flow model — are better than theory, but they cannot contradict the model the way a recording can, so those voices sit at 2 and say why.

The class column is what the ROUTER returns, not the program map. Several programs are a family routed per note — the bowed and pizzicato ensembles, the brass section, the solo winds whose bottom octave is a different instrument — and reading the program map instead reports the no-note fallback. That is how GM 61 came to be listed as a trombone standing in for a whole section long after it had been routed to trumpet, trombone and tuba sections.

For a synth program the instrument is a synthesizer. The 32 synth programs — basses, strings, brass, leads, pads, effects and the synth drum — are patches on a Moog Messenger, and the Messenger engine is fitted against Ben’s own Messenger, recorded through a mixer and back (examples/messenger_fit.py): every knob law the patches use, from the cutoff and the contours to the waveshape, the FM index, LFO 1 and the output stage. Whole notes, attack to release, agree with the hardware within 0.6–2 dB on 28 of the 32. That is a recording of the instrument, so a patch that is the Messenger alone is a 4; one with a part no Messenger has — vocal formants after it, the guitars’ valve, the MF-104M pedal, which is modelled and not recorded — is a 3. And the reference runs both ways: a patch’s knobs are the hardware’s, so it plays on the Messenger itself.

Every one of the 128 programs now has a voice of its own kind; what remains is how much reality each has been allowed to contradict. None is played by a voice of the wrong physical kind: the last three were 123 Bird Tweet, 124 Telephone Ring and 125 Helicopter.
